# Two Planetary Shocks, One Interdependent World
## Nuclear Winter, Climate Change and Hydrometeorological Disasters: Physical Mechanisms, Cascading Risks, Evidence Limits and Resilience Priorities

**Research type:** Narrative evidence synthesis  
**Project date:** 28 October 2026  
**Status:** Preprint / educational research build; not peer reviewed

## Abstract

**Background.** Nuclear war and anthropogenic climate change are distinct drivers of planetary risk. Nuclear winter research examines abrupt climate disruption after soot from large fires enters the upper atmosphere; climate-change research examines the evolving probability, intensity and consequences of heat, heavy precipitation, drought, storms and coastal flooding. Their physical mechanisms and timescales differ, but both can destabilize connected food, water, energy, health and transport systems.

**Approach.** This paper synthesizes selected peer-reviewed nuclear-winter and food-system modelling studies with IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) assessments, the World Meteorological Organization’s State of the Global Climate 2025, and UN disaster-risk terminology. It compares causal pathways, evidence confidence, key assumptions and intervention points. A companion interactive scenario lab uses published nuclear-winter benchmark values and a separately labelled conceptual exposure-vulnerability index.

**Findings.** Published model experiments indicate that sufficiently large soot injections can sharply reduce surface sunlight, cool the climate, disrupt precipitation and reduce crop and marine food production for years. IPCC assessments find that heavy precipitation has intensified across many well-observed land regions and that climate-related water risks increase with warming, while regional drought and flood outcomes depend on local climate, hydrology and human systems. In both domains, aggregate hazard metrics alone cannot determine societal outcomes: trade, distribution, infrastructure, inequality and the speed of response mediate losses.

**Conclusion.** The strongest practical strategy is to reduce exposure and vulnerability through risk-informed land use, early warning, resilient critical infrastructure, diversified food supply, water management, health-system readiness, emissions mitigation and international nuclear-risk reduction. The simulator is an educational stress test, not a validated forecast. Decision-grade projections require location-specific data and models that explicitly represent the relevant physical and social mechanisms.

**Keywords:** nuclear winter; black carbon; hydroclimatic extremes; flood; drought; food security; compound risk; climate adaptation; disaster risk reduction.

## 1. Research questions and scope

1. How do soot-driven nuclear-winter climate disruptions differ from climate-change-driven hydrometeorological hazards?
2. Which pathways transmit physical hazards into food insecurity, water stress, health impacts, infrastructure failures and displacement?
3. Which quantitative results are directly supported by published studies, and where do assumptions or uncertainty prevent a precise local forecast?
4. Which resilience interventions reduce impacts across more than one plausible shock scenario?

The scope is global, with additional attention to South and Central Asia and the Hindu Kush Himalaya (HKH), where monsoon variability, glacier and snow changes, steep topography and dependence on interconnected river basins produce a complex hazard context. This paper is not a full systematic review, new climate simulation, event-attribution study or country-level disaster forecast.

## 2. Methods: evidence selection and synthesis

This project uses a transparent narrative synthesis of authoritative assessments and selected peer-reviewed papers. Sources were selected for relevance to (a) nuclear-winter atmospheric and crop/fisheries modelling, (b) observed and projected hydrometeorological extremes, (c) water-related risk and adaptation, and (d) current global climate indicators. Priority was given to primary research and assessment bodies, including the IPCC, WMO, Nature Food, the *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres* and UNDRR.

The synthesis follows four steps:

1. **Physical mechanisms:** describe forcing, atmospheric response, water/energy changes and affected systems.
2. **Evidence tagging:** separate reported model results, assessment statements and conceptual illustrations.
3. **Pathway comparison:** trace indirect effects through critical infrastructure and social systems.
4. **Uncertainty review:** record model limitations, regional heterogeneity and social-response assumptions.

Search and source verification were performed for the project build on 28 October 2026. The included evidence is a focused selection, not an exhaustive systematic literature search. No new gridded climate data, casualty records, econometric models or crop-model outputs were produced. The review also incorporates Harrison et al. (2026), an open-access data article describing standardized model-output datasets covering temperature, precipitation, ultraviolet radiation, agriculture, fish catch and sea ice.

## 3. Results and synthesis

### 3.1 Nuclear winter: an abrupt soot-mediated shock

Coupe et al. (2019) compared comprehensive climate-model simulations for a 150 teragram (Tg) soot scenario for a large US–Russia nuclear war. Both the newer WACCM4 model and the earlier ModelE simulation produced severe cooling because stratospheric smoke reduced incoming solar radiation. The newer model did not remove the large-scale response, and its first few years produced larger temperature and precipitation reductions. The paper identifies substantial uncertainty in emitted soot amount, injection height and aerosol lifetime.

Xia et al. (2022) linked climate-model outputs to crop and marine fishery models and then assessed national food calories under different post-war assumptions. They examined six soot-injection scenarios from 5 to 150 Tg. Their reported results include approximately a **7% global annual crop-calorie reduction in years 1–5 for 5 Tg**, **up to a 50% reduction in the 47 Tg scenario**, and **around a 90% crop-calorie reduction in years 3–4 for the 150 Tg scenario**. These are outputs of specific model scenarios, not universally agreed predictions for every nuclear conflict.

The food-security estimate is mediated by assumptions about trade, livestock conversion, food waste, alternative crops, distribution and calorie access. The authors identify limitations including crop responses to a suddenly colder climate, irrigation assumptions, insects and pollinators, and the need for additional multi-model work. Famine-death estimates in the paper should therefore be quoted as scenario-dependent model estimates, not deterministic counts.

**Evidence distinction:** cooling from soot injection is supported by model physics and published experiments. Exact global famine deaths or local food availability are less certain because they depend on physical scenario choices and human-response assumptions.

### 3.2 Climate change alters hydrological risk

IPCC AR6 Working Group I Chapter 11 concludes that heavy precipitation events have likely increased in frequency and intensity over the majority of land regions with good observational coverage. Human influence, particularly greenhouse-gas emissions, is assessed as the main driver of observed global-scale intensification of heavy precipitation over land. Attribution and confidence vary by region, event type and drought metric; a global trend does not prove that climate change alone caused a specific disaster.

IPCC AR6 Working Group II Chapter 4 assesses that intensification of the hydrological cycle affects water security, drought and flood risks increase with each degree of warming, and direct flood damages at 4°C global warming may be four to five times those at 1.5°C, with medium confidence. This is a broad assessment result and must not be applied as a precise multiplier to an individual river, city, asset or insurance portfolio.

WMO’s *State of the Global Climate 2025*, released in March 2026, reports 2015–2025 as the warmest eleven-year period on record and places 2025 at approximately **1.43 ± 0.13°C above the 1850–1900 average**. It also describes record-high ocean heat content and an Earth energy imbalance at the high end of the observational record. These indicators are context for the changing climate system, not direct predictions of any one hazard.

### 3.3 South Asian risk is compound and heterogeneous

IPCC AR6 WGII Chapter 10 describes increasing heatwave threats across Asia, drought risks in arid and semi-arid areas of West, Central and South Asia, floods in monsoon regions, and glacier melting in the Hindu Kush Himalaya. It discusses flash floods, debris flows, landslides, avalanches and changes to river systems connected to snow, ice and permafrost. Topography, reservoir operation, land cover, infrastructure, sediment and settlement patterns can be decisive; the hazard signal alone is insufficient to infer losses.

The region illustrates why local adaptation should combine meteorological forecasts with hydrological monitoring, slope and watershed management, resilient road and bridge networks, river-basin coordination, water storage and demand management, heat-health plans and inclusive warning dissemination. A regional assessment cannot establish a site-specific return period or assign the cause of a particular landslide without event and site evidence.

## 4. Comparative framework

| Dimension | Nuclear-winter pathway | Climate-change / hydrometeorological pathway |
|---|---|---|
| Primary driver | War, urban/industrial firestorms and stratospheric soot | Human-caused warming interacting with natural variability and local conditions |
| Onset and duration | Acute event; modelled climate and food impacts may persist for years | Long-term forcing with episodic hazards, trends and compound events |
| Key physical mechanisms | Reduced solar radiation, cooling, precipitation changes, growing-season stress | Heat extremes, altered heavy precipitation, drought, sea-level rise and cyclone-related risks |
| Food pathway | Crop, fishery and feed production disrupted by sudden climate shock | Heat, rainfall extremes, drought, water availability and coastal impacts affect production and distribution |
| Critical uncertainties | Soot mass, injection altitude, aerosol microphysics, war scenario and trade response | Emissions pathway, internal variability, regional downscaling, exposure and adaptive capacity |
| Shared intervention types | Food contingency planning, robust supply chains, public-health readiness and nuclear-risk reduction | Early warning, resilient infrastructure, risk-informed land use, adaptation and mitigation |

This comparison is conceptual. It does not imply that ordinary climate change can reproduce nuclear winter or that preparedness for floods can address direct nuclear detonations.

## 5. Cascading risk: from physical shock to social crisis

Five interacting pathways deserve explicit attention.

1. **Food and markets.** Crop losses, transport disruption, export controls and price spikes can translate physical shocks into unequal calorie access. National production totals conceal within-country inequality.
2. **Water and sanitation.** Floods can contaminate water and overwhelm wastewater systems; drought and heat can reduce availability and increase demand. Energy failure can further affect pumping and treatment.
3. **Energy and communications.** Extreme heat increases electricity demand while storms and flooding can disable lines, substations, roads and telecom infrastructure. Service failures may interrupt warnings, medicine refrigeration and water treatment.
4. **Health and livelihoods.** Heat stress, injury, food insecurity, waterborne disease, loss of income and interrupted routine care can compound, disproportionately affecting people with fewer resources.
5. **Governance and displacement.** Simultaneous demands on emergency services, logistics, public finance and social trust can slow response. Displacement and migration are shaped by protection, work, housing and policy choices, not by climate alone.

These pathways are grounded at a broad level, but their probability, magnitude and timing in a particular event require event-specific modelling. They should be treated as causal pathways to investigate, not as a guaranteed domino sequence.

## 6. Resilience priorities

### 6.1 Protect lifelines first
Map dependencies across electricity, water, hospitals, communications, fuel, transport and food logistics. Use backup power, interoperable communications, redundant routes and tested continuity plans.

### 6.2 Make warnings actionable
Connect monitoring to locally understood thresholds, multilingual alerts, evacuation support and pre-agreed action. Evaluate lead time, reach, trust and protection for people with disabilities and limited connectivity.

### 6.3 Build food-system buffers
Diversify suppliers, protect seed and input access, reduce avoidable loss, maintain transparent contingency reserves and plan equitable distribution under multi-year disruption.

### 6.4 Reduce water and heat exposure
Improve drainage where justified, restore catchments and wetlands where appropriate, monitor groundwater and river systems, secure clean-water backups, and establish heat-health plans and cooling access.

### 6.5 Prevent the preventable
Rapid greenhouse-gas emissions reductions limit future climate risk. Distinct nuclear-risk measures include diplomacy, crisis communication, arms control, nuclear safeguards, non-use commitments and strengthened international cooperation.

### 6.6 Fund equitable adaptation
Prioritize risk-exposed communities, basic services, resilient housing, public health, local data and social protection. Track who is protected, not only aggregate investment.

## 7. Limitations and research agenda

This is a narrative synthesis, not a registered systematic review or meta-analysis. It did not execute CESM, WACCM, crop, fisheries, hydrological or economic models; literature outputs are reported as those authors’ results. The website's intermediate soot values are interpolations between selected published anchors and are not runs of the referenced models. Its preparedness/exposure composite is pedagogical and not calibrated to observed casualties, losses or local risk.

Global warming levels are not local weather forecasts. Local flood, drought, heat and landslide analysis needs downscaled climate data, terrain, land cover, hydrology and infrastructure/exposure datasets. Event attribution requires counterfactual analysis and event-specific evidence; a hazard occurring in a warming climate is not by itself proof that climate change caused that event. Results and confidence can vary with assumptions, model versions and future peer-reviewed research.

Priority research agenda:

1. Compare multi-model nuclear-winter climate ensembles and quantify uncertainty in fire emissions, soot properties, atmospheric lifetime and regional monsoons.
2. Couple climate outputs to multi-model crop, fishery, livestock, storage, trade, price and household-access models, including intra-country inequality.
3. Build regional hydroclimate stress tests integrating climate, river basins, landslides, infrastructure, population, early-warning coverage and adaptation capacity.
4. Develop transparent compound-risk models validated against historic extremes and evaluated with stakeholder input.
5. Measure resilience interventions by avoided losses, distributional outcomes, service continuity and access for vulnerable populations.

## 8. Conclusion

Nuclear winter and climate-driven hydrometeorological disasters arise from different physical drivers. Their societal consequences are nevertheless shaped by interdependence: food systems require energy and transport; water services require power and infrastructure; health depends on clean water, stable supply chains and functioning communications. A useful research programme must link physical hazards to social and economic response while exposing the assumptions at every step.

The evidence supports urgent action on climate mitigation and adaptation, disaster risk reduction, resilient critical services, equitable early warning, food-system contingency planning and nuclear-risk prevention. The exact outcome of a future shock cannot be known from a single chart. Better decisions come from evidence, transparent models, local data, multiple scenarios and preparation that protects the people most likely to be left behind.
