PATHWAY A / ABRUPT SOOT INJECTION

Nuclear winter:
from sunlight to sustenance.

In published simulations, soot from widespread urban and industrial fires can absorb sunlight, warm the upper atmosphere and cool the surface. Magnitude depends strongly on how much soot reaches the stratosphere and how it evolves.

PUBLISHED MODEL RESULT
5–150 Tg

Scenarios, not a forecast

Xia et al. modelled six stratospheric soot-injection cases. Results are highly scenario-dependent; Tg means teragram, or one million metric tonnes.

Disruption can outlast the event

The climate disruptions in the scenarios persist for about a decade, with the largest effects in the first several years. This is a modelled timescale, not a precise recovery clock.

Food access is not food output

Crop losses become famine through distribution, trade, stockpiles, prices, conflict, nutrition and policy. Models must assume how societies respond, and those assumptions shape outcomes.

PATHWAY B / WATER & ENERGY EXTREMES

Hydrometeorological risk:
the water cycle under pressure.

Hydrometeorological hazards have atmospheric, hydrological or oceanographic origins. Climate change alters the hazard landscape, but disaster outcomes also depend on drainage, land use, settlement patterns, warning lead time, building quality and social protection.

≈
01 / TOO MUCH WATER

Heavy rain & floods

A warmer atmosphere can hold more moisture. Heavy precipitation has increased in many well-observed land regions, while local flood impacts depend on terrain, drainage, soil, river engineering and exposure.

02 / TOO LITTLE WATER

Drought & water stress

Changes in rainfall, heat and evaporation interact with water demand and storage. Drought trends differ by region and drought type, so a global statement is not a local forecast.

03 / EXCESS HEAT

Heatwaves & health

Extreme heat increases health risks and can stress power systems, outdoor work, crops, ecosystems and cooling access. Risk is amplified by urban heat islands and unequal access to protection.

04 / COMPOUND COAST

Cyclones & storm surge

Sea-level rise raises the baseline for coastal flooding. Rainfall, wind, waves, storm surge and exposed coastal assets can compound, with outcomes shaped by local geography and adaptation.
